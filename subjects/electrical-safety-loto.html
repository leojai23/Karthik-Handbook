Electrical Safety Reference

Electrical Safety & LOTO

A guided path from how electrical current actually injures, through approach boundaries and PPE, into the lockout/tagout procedure that makes every panel and machine in this Handbook safe to actually work on. This is a reference for building understanding, not a substitute for your site's own regulatory training and arc-flash study — see the callouts throughout for exactly where that line sits.

Before anything else This tutorial explains the concepts behind electrical safety procedures. It does not qualify you to perform lockout/tagout or energized work, does not replace your employer's site-specific LOTO procedures (which OSHA 1910.147 and equivalent regulations require), and must never be used in place of a proper, qualified arc-flash hazard study for real PPE selection. Where your local regulation or site procedure differs from anything described here, your site procedure governs.

● Basic

Why These Procedures Exist

Hazard

Every panel, PLC, VFD, and relay board covered elsewhere in this Handbook eventually has a live wire in it that a person has to work near. Electrical safety procedures — de-energizing before work, lockout/tagout, approach boundaries, PPE — exist because the two main hazards (electric shock and arc flash) are both entirely preventable with a correctly followed procedure, and both capable of killing or permanently injuring someone when the procedure is skipped. This isn't abstract caution; it's the specific, learnable discipline that turns "working on a panel" from a genuine hazard into a routine, safe task.

Shock Hazards: How Current Injures

Hazard

It's current through the body, not voltage alone, that causes injury — which is exactly why even "low voltage" 24VDC control circuits aren't automatically safe to treat carelessly if the path and conditions are right, though the real danger in most industrial settings comes from mains and higher voltages. Commonly cited illustrative thresholds for AC current through the chest (these vary by individual, current path, and duration, so treat them as illustrative, not precise cutoffs):

Current (AC, through chest)Effect
≈0.5–1mAthreshold of perception — a tingle
≈1–5mAthreshold of pain
≈10–20mA"let-go" threshold — above this, muscles can clamp involuntarily, making it impossible to release the source
≈20mA+respiratory muscle paralysis risk
≈100–500mAventricular fibrillation risk — often fatal without immediate defibrillation

The let-go threshold is the detail most worth remembering: at currents above roughly 10–20mA, a person may be physically unable to release what's shocking them — which is exactly why prolonged contact, not just contact itself, is so dangerous, and why procedures are built around never being in contact in the first place rather than relying on being able to pull away.

PPE Basics

Hazard

Personal Protective Equipment for electrical work addresses two distinct hazards with different gear: shock protection (insulated gloves rated and tested for the voltage involved, insulated tools) and arc flash protection (flame-resistant clothing, face shields or hoods, rated by arc rating in cal/cm² — see Advanced below). Critically, PPE is the last line of defense, not the first — it reduces injury severity if something goes wrong, but the actual safety plan is de-energizing and verifying zero energy (Basic/Intermediate below) so that PPE's protection is never actually needed in the first place.

De-Energized Work: the Default

Procedure

The governing principle underneath every section in this tutorial: equipment should be de-energized and locked out before anyone works on it, as the default, not the exception. Energized work is permitted only in narrow, specifically justified circumstances (see Energized Work Permits below) — never as a convenience to avoid the time cost of a proper shutdown and lockout.

● Intermediate

Lockout/Tagout: the Six Steps

Procedure
  1. Preparation — identify every energy source feeding the equipment, and review the specific written LOTO procedure for it.
  2. Shutdown — stop the equipment using its normal operating controls.
  3. Isolation — physically operate every energy-isolating device (breaker, disconnect switch, valve) needed to disconnect every identified source.
  4. Lock & Tag — apply a lock to each isolating device, holding it in the safe position, with a tag identifying who applied it and why.
  5. Release Stored Energy — discharge capacitors, bleed pneumatic/hydraulic pressure, block or lower suspended loads — every stored-energy form the equipment carries, not just the source just isolated.
  6. Verify Zero Energy — before touching anything, confirm de-energization: attempt a normal start (nothing should happen) and/or test with a properly rated meter.

These steps happen in this order every time, with no shortcuts — skipping straight to step 4 without properly completing step 3 (fully identifying and isolating every source) is exactly how someone ends up locking out the obvious power feed while a second, forgotten source stays live.

Identifying All Energy Sources

Procedure

"Electrical" is rarely the only energy source on real industrial equipment. The VFD tutorial's braking-methods section is a direct, concrete example already in this Handbook: a VFD's DC bus capacitors store meaningful energy that persists after the main breaker is opened, sometimes for a significant discharge time — turning off the breaker alone, without accounting for that stored capacitor energy, is an incomplete lockout. The Energy Source Worksheet above walks through the full checklist: electrical (both line power and stored capacitor/DC bus energy), pneumatic, hydraulic, mechanical (springs, flywheels), gravity (anything that could fall), thermal, and chemical — a complete LOTO procedure accounts for every one that applies to the specific equipment, not just the most obvious one.

Group Lockout & Multiple Workers

Procedure

When more than one person works on the same locked-out equipment, each individual applies their own personal lock — commonly through a lockout hasp, a device that lets multiple padlocks all block the same isolating point simultaneously. The equipment can only be re-energized once every single worker has removed their own lock, which means no one worker's departure (or mistake) can leave another worker exposed; nobody else's lock can substitute for your own.

Energized Work Permits

Procedure

A narrow set of tasks genuinely cannot be done de-energized — diagnostic voltage/current measurements, and a small list of similarly justified exceptions — and these require a specific, documented energized work permit process: a qualified person, with the task's necessity explicitly justified (not merely "it's faster"), using properly rated PPE and test equipment, within the approach boundaries covered above. The existence of a formal permit process is itself the safeguard against energized work becoming a convenience shortcut rather than the rare, deliberate exception it's meant to be.

Verification: Proving Zero Energy

Hazard

Step 6 of LOTO deserves its own emphasis: verification is not optional, and not satisfied merely by having completed the lockout steps correctly — it's a separate, explicit test. The standard approach is "test before you touch": attempt to start the equipment using its normal controls (confirming nothing happens), and/or directly measure voltage with a correctly rated test instrument (see Test Equipment: CAT Ratings in Advanced) at the actual point of work, not just at the isolating device. A lock correctly applied to the wrong breaker, or a second unidentified source, are both real, if less common, failure modes that only this verification step catches.

● Advanced

Arc Flash: What Actually Happens

Hazard

An arc flash is a short circuit through air rather than through a conductor — current ionizes a path and sustains an electrical arc, releasing an enormous burst of thermal energy, pressure, and light in a fraction of a second. It is not "just a spark": temperatures can exceed the surface of the sun, the pressure wave can be forceful enough to injure on its own, and the incident energy delivered to anyone nearby is measured in the same cal/cm² units used to rate PPE below. Available fault current and how quickly the protective device clears the fault (its clearing time) are the two biggest factors determining how severe a given arc flash event is — which is exactly why the number on an equipment's arc flash label is specific to that equipment's actual electrical system, not a generic constant.

PPE Categories & Incident Energy

Hazard

NFPA 70E's table method assigns a PPE Category based on incident energy, each with a minimum arc rating the protective clothing must meet:

CategoryMinimum arc rating
14 cal/cm²
28 cal/cm²
325 cal/cm²
440 cal/cm²

The arc flash boundary itself is defined as the distance at which incident energy reaches 1.2 cal/cm² — the threshold for a second-degree burn on exposed skin.

What this tutorial deliberately does not provide Calculating actual incident energy for a real piece of equipment requires a qualified engineering study (per NFPA 70E / IEEE 1584 methodology) using site-specific data: available bolted fault current, protective device clearing time, working distance, equipment class, and more. This tutorial intentionally does not offer an incident-energy calculator — a simplified formula here would invite exactly the kind of false confidence that leads to the wrong PPE being selected for real equipment. Always use your facility's current, professionally prepared arc-flash study and labels.

Reading Arc Flash Labels

Procedure

A compliant arc flash label, already affixed to real equipment, is the practical artifact this tutorial's concepts actually serve: it typically states the nominal voltage, the incident energy (or directly, the PPE category), the arc flash boundary distance, the limited/restricted approach boundaries, and the minimum PPE required. Reading and correctly interpreting that label — not performing the underlying calculation yourself — is the skill that matters day-to-day for anyone approaching labeled equipment.

Test Equipment: CAT Ratings

Hazard

Multimeters and voltage testers carry a CAT rating (CAT I–IV, per IEC 61010) describing how large a voltage transient the instrument can safely withstand at a given point in an electrical system — not just what voltage it can display. The category number matters as much as the voltage rating:

CategoryTypical location
CAT Iprotected electronic circuits
CAT IIplug-in loads — appliances, chargers, bench equipment
CAT IIIbuilding fixed wiring — distribution panels, industrial equipment, three-phase motors
CAT IVservice entrance / utility connection point

Using a CAT II-rated meter (sized for a wall outlet) on CAT III distribution panel wiring is a real, dangerous mismatch — the meter can fail violently under a transient it wasn't built to survive, becoming a hazard itself. Always match the meter's CAT rating (and voltage rating within that category) to where it's actually being used, not just to the voltage being measured.

Qualified vs Unqualified Persons

Procedure

"Qualified person" is a specific regulatory status, not a self-assessment — someone trained and demonstrably capable of recognizing and avoiding the specific electrical hazards of the equipment they're working on, per their employer's documented determination. This status gates real things throughout this tutorial: who may enter the restricted approach boundary, who may be issued an energized work permit, who performs the isolation steps of LOTO. An unqualified person can still safely be present (escorted, outside the restricted boundary, following directions) but shouldn't perform the hazard-adjacent steps themselves until that status is formally established through training specific to their role and equipment.

Safety in the Panels This Handbook Describes

Procedure

Pulling this back to the rest of the Handbook: the Control Panel Design tutorial's worked panel example — PLC, VFD, Modbus relay board, ESP32 gateway, all in one enclosure — is exactly the kind of equipment this tutorial's procedures apply to directly. Locking it out means isolating line power at the main disconnect and accounting for the VFD's DC bus capacitor discharge time, and isolating any separately-fed 24VDC control supply, and verifying zero energy at each of those points before anyone opens the panel door to troubleshoot the Modbus wiring or re-terminate a relay. None of the individual steps are complicated; the discipline, as with everything else in this Handbook's panel-build content, is applying all of them correctly, every time, without shortcuts.

● Practice Exercises
Basic Identify why the let-go threshold matters

Why is the "let-go threshold" (roughly 10–20mA) considered such an important number in electrical safety, beyond simply being "a current level"?

Answer Above this threshold, involuntary muscle contraction can make it physically impossible for a person to release the energized conductor on their own — turning a brief contact into prolonged contact, which dramatically increases injury severity and the risk of progressing toward ventricular fibrillation at higher currents. It's a key reason procedures are built around never making contact at all, rather than relying on the ability to let go.
Basic Distinguish a lock from a tag

In lockout/tagout, what's the functional difference between the "lock" and the "tag," and why does OSHA's procedure require both rather than either alone?

Answer The lock is a physical barrier — the isolating device genuinely cannot be moved back to "on" while it's in place. The tag is informational — it identifies who applied the lockout and why, but doesn't physically prevent anyone from bypassing it. A tag alone only warns; a lock alone (with no identification) makes troubleshooting who's working and why much harder. Together, one physically prevents re-energization and the other communicates the reason.
Intermediate Spot an incomplete lockout

A technician locks out the main breaker feeding a VFD-driven motor panel and begins work immediately. What critical step have they likely skipped, and why does it matter specifically for this equipment?

Answer They've skipped releasing stored energy — specifically, allowing the VFD's DC bus capacitors to discharge. Opening the main breaker removes incoming power, but the capacitors can retain a dangerous charge for some time afterward; touching DC bus terminals before that stored energy is released or verified safe is a real hazard the breaker lockout alone doesn't address.
Intermediate Choose the correct meter for the job

A technician needs to verify zero voltage on a building's main distribution panel. They have a CAT II-rated meter rated for a higher voltage than the panel's nominal voltage. Is this meter appropriate? Why or why not?

Answer No. CAT II is rated for plug-in loads, not building distribution wiring — that's CAT III territory (or CAT IV at the service entrance). Having a sufficiently high voltage rating doesn't substitute for the correct category; the category describes the transient energy the meter can survive at that point in the system, and a CAT II meter used on CAT III wiring can fail dangerously even if its voltage number looks adequate.
Advanced Explain why the arc flash boundary isn't the same as the shock approach boundaries

Why does NFPA 70E define the arc flash boundary separately from the limited and restricted approach boundaries, rather than using one single "safe distance"?

Answer They protect against different hazards with different physics. The limited/restricted approach boundaries govern shock risk and scale primarily with voltage. The arc flash boundary governs burn risk from an arc flash event and scales with available fault current and clearing time — factors that can make the arc flash boundary closer or farther than the shock boundaries depending on the specific equipment, which is exactly why real labels list both separately rather than one combined number.
Advanced Justify why this tutorial avoids an incident-energy calculator

Other tutorials in this Handbook include live calculators for everything from CRC-16 checksums to voltage drop. Why does this tutorial deliberately not include an arc-flash incident-energy calculator?

Answer A real incident-energy calculation requires a qualified engineering study using site-specific data (available fault current, protective device clearing time, working distance, equipment class) per NFPA 70E/IEEE 1584 methodology — it isn't a simple formula that can be safely approximated by a generic web tool. Offering a simplified calculator here would risk someone trusting its output for real PPE selection, which could directly contribute to inadequate protection in a genuinely hazardous situation. Some calculations are appropriate to approximate for learning; this one isn't, given the stakes.
