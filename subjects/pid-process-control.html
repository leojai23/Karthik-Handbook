● Basic

What a P&ID Is (and Isn't)

Process

A Piping and Instrumentation Diagram documents, in detail, every pipe, vessel, valve, and instrument in a process and how they connect — the single source of truth an operator, maintenance technician, or engineer uses to understand exactly what's physically and electrically present. It's more detailed than a Process Flow Diagram (PFD), which shows the conceptual process flow and major equipment without every valve and instrument (see PFD vs P&ID vs Loop Diagram in Advanced), and less detailed than a loop diagram, which drills into one single control loop's actual terminal-by-terminal wiring.

Equipment Symbols

These equipment symbols are the "nouns" of a P&ID; the lines and instrument bubbles covered next are what turn them into a readable sentence describing the actual process.

Line Types: Piping vs Signals

Instrumentation

The line style between symbols tells you what kind of connection it is — this is the detail that lets a P&ID pack an enormous amount of information into one drawing without needing a label on every single line:

Line styleRepresents
Thick solid linemain process piping — the actual product/fluid path
Thin solid lineinstrument connection to the process (how a sensor taps into the pipe/vessel)
Dashed lineelectrical signal — a 4–20mA loop, HART, discrete I/O (see the Electronics & Instrumentation tutorial's current-loop section for exactly what's carried on this line)
Dashed line with diagonal cross-hatchespneumatic signal — instrument air driving a valve actuator
Dashed line with small circles (o—o)software/data link — an internal DCS/PLC connection, not a physical wire

Instrument Bubbles: Location

Instrumentation

Every instrument is drawn as a circle ("bubble" or "balloon"), and the lines drawn through or around that circle encode where it's physically located and how its reading is displayed:

Bubble styleLocation
Plain circle, no linefield-mounted — physically out in the process area, at the equipment
Circle with a single solid horizontal lineprimary location, normally accessible to the operator (a control room DCS screen or a front-of-panel display)
Circle with a double horizontal linepanel-mounted but not normally accessible — rear-of-panel or a local panel, not the main control room
Hexagona computer/DCS function — the control algorithm lives in software, not as a discrete physical instrument
Square with a diagonal crossa PLC function, per the ISA-5.1/IEC convention distinguishing PLC logic from DCS logic

Instrument Tag Numbers

Instrumentation

Every instrument's bubble carries a tag — a first letter for the measured variable, one or more succeeding letters for the function, and a loop number tying every device in one control loop together:

First lettersSucceeding letters
FFlowTTransmit
TTemperatureIIndicate
PPressureCControl
LLevelSSwitch
AAnalysisVValve (final control element)

Worked example: LT-101 is a Level Transmitter on loop 101; LIC-101 (same loop) is a Level Indicating Controller; LV-101 (same loop again) is the Level-controlled Valve. Reading the tag alone already tells you what the device measures or does, before looking at its symbol at all. Try building your own in the Tag Decoder above.

Control Valves: Fail Open vs Fail Closed

Process

A control valve's symbol on a P&ID typically notes its fail-safe position — FO (fails open) or FC (fails closed) — the position the valve mechanically springs to if it loses actuating power or signal. This is a deliberate safety design choice, exactly the same fail-safe philosophy as the Electrical Safety tutorial's "energized to run" wiring convention: a cooling-water valve to a reactor should typically be FO (losing signal shouldn't also lose cooling), while a valve feeding fuel to a burner should typically be FC (losing signal should stop fuel flow, not leave it flowing unchecked).

Loop Diagrams: One Level Deeper

Instrumentation

Where a P&ID shows that LT-101 connects to LIC-101 connects to LV-101, a loop diagram shows exactly how — terminal numbers, wire numbers, cable types, junction boxes, power supply sources, down to the specific PLC I/O card and channel each device lands on. This is the direct instrumentation-side equivalent of the Control Panel Design tutorial's wire numbering and terminal block documentation, scoped to one complete control loop rather than one physical panel — the document a technician actually uses when troubleshooting why LIC-101 isn't reading correctly.

Alarms & Interlocks

Instrumentation

An alarm (LAH-101, Level Alarm High) informs the operator that a value has crossed a threshold — it demands attention but takes no automatic action on its own. An interlock automatically takes a protective action (closing a valve, stopping a pump) when a condition is met, with or without operator involvement — the direct process-side equivalent of the PLC Learning tutorial's safety interlocking concept. A well-documented P&ID distinguishes these clearly, because confusing "the operator will be warned" with "the system will automatically protect itself" is exactly the kind of gap that matters when something actually goes wrong.

PFD vs P&ID vs Loop Diagram

Process

Three documents, increasing in detail, each serving a different audience and purpose:

DocumentShowsUsed by
PFD (Process Flow Diagram)major equipment, overall flow, key stream data (temperatures, pressures, flow rates at design conditions) — conceptual, not every valve/instrumentprocess engineers, early design, management overviews
P&IDevery pipe, valve, vessel, and instrument, with tags and line types as covered throughout this tutorialoperators, maintenance, control engineers — the day-to-day reference
Loop diagramone single loop's complete wiring, terminal-by-terminalinstrumentation technicians troubleshooting that specific loop

Each document deliberately omits detail the others carry — a PFD overloaded with every instrument tag would be unreadable at the level it's meant to communicate, and a P&ID without instrument tags would be useless for the troubleshooting job it exists to support.

Control Philosophy & Narrative

Process

Before (and alongside) the P&ID's symbols and tags, a control narrative (or control philosophy document) describes, in plain prose, the intended control strategy: what each loop is supposed to do, under what conditions, and why. The P&ID is the implementation of that narrative in symbolic form; when a loop's behaviour seems wrong, the narrative is often the faster place to check first — "is the equipment doing what the P&ID shows?" versus "is the P&ID even showing what was intended?" are two different questions, and the narrative answers the second.

Redundancy & Voting

Instrumentation

Safety-critical measurements sometimes use multiple redundant transmitters with a voting scheme — a common notation is 2oo3 ("two out of three"): three independent transmitters measure the same variable, and the safety system acts only when at least two agree a trip condition exists, protecting against both a single transmitter failing low (falsely triggering a trip) and a single transmitter failing high (masking a real hazard). This sits squarely in Safety Instrumented System (SIS) territory — a deeper, more specialized field than this tutorial covers, but the voting concept itself is worth recognising on sight when it appears on a P&ID.

Revision Control & As-Builts

Process

Exactly the same discipline as the Control Panel Design tutorial's as-built documentation section, applied to the process side: a P&ID drawn during design rarely matches the plant exactly as commissioned and subsequently modified, and an out-of-date P&ID is actively dangerous — a technician trusting it during lockout/tagout (see the Electrical Safety tutorial) could isolate the wrong valve entirely if a field modification was never reflected back into the drawing. P&IDs carry revision numbers and dates for exactly this reason, and a disciplined site treats "update the P&ID" as part of making any physical change, not an optional afterthought.

Reading Real-World Complexity

Process

Real plants have dozens of loops with similar functions, so loop numbers are often structured rather than arbitrary — an area or unit prefix (e.g. loops 100–199 for Unit 1, 200–299 for Unit 2) keeps related equipment grouped and makes a tag's rough location inferable from its number alone. Large P&IDs also span multiple sheets, with cross-reference symbols (a line exiting one sheet with a note pointing to the continuing sheet and drawing number) showing where a pipe or signal physically continues — reading a real multi-sheet P&ID set fluently just means following that same tag-and-line discipline across sheet boundaries rather than assuming everything fits on one page.

From P&ID to PLC Program

Instrumentation

Closing the loop back to the rest of this Handbook: LIC-101 on a P&ID isn't an abstract symbol — it's a specific PLC analog input (reading LT-101's scaled value, exactly the Analog I/O scaling covered in the PLC Learning tutorial), a PID instruction block (the same PID concept introduced in the Electronics and PLC tutorials, now with a concrete worked loop to tune), and an analog output driving LV-101. Reading a P&ID fluently is, in a real sense, reading the specification for what the PLC program still needs to implement — the documentation and the code are two views of the exact same control loop.

● Practice Exercises
Basic Decode an instrument tag

What does the tag PT-205 represent?

Answer A Pressure Transmitter on loop 205. Confirm it in the Tag Decoder above.
Basic Identify a line type

A P&ID shows a dashed line with small diagonal cross-hatches running from a controller to a valve actuator. What does this line represent?

Answer A pneumatic signal — instrument air driving the valve actuator, as opposed to a plain dashed line (electrical) or a dashed line with circles (software link).
Intermediate Interpret a bubble's location notation

An instrument on a P&ID is drawn as a circle with a single solid horizontal line through it. What does this tell you about where the operator sees its reading?

Answer It's in the primary location, normally accessible to the operator — a control room DCS screen or front-of-panel display, as opposed to a plain circle (field-mounted, no remote display) or a double-line circle (panel-mounted but not normally accessible).
Intermediate Choose a valve's fail-safe position

A control valve regulates cooling water flow into a reactor that will overheat dangerously if cooling is lost. Should this valve be specified fail-open (FO) or fail-closed (FC), and why?

Answer Fail-open (FO). If the valve loses its actuating signal or power, it must default to a safe state — here, that means cooling continues rather than stopping, since loss of cooling is the dangerous condition. This mirrors the same "energized to run, de-energized to stop safely" logic from the Electrical Safety tutorial's fail-safe wiring discussion.
Advanced Walk through a complete control loop

A P&ID shows FT-301 (field-mounted) → FIC-301 (circle with single line) → FV-301 on a feed line into a mixing vessel, with FV-301 marked FC. Describe what this loop does and its safety behaviour in plain English.

Answer A flow transmitter at the field (FT-301) measures flow into the vessel and sends it electrically to a flow indicating controller visible to the operator (FIC-301), which compares it to a setpoint and adjusts a control valve (FV-301) on the feed line to hold that flow rate. Because the valve is fail-closed, losing signal or actuating power stops the feed flow entirely — the safe default for a feed stream into a vessel, preventing uncontrolled overfilling or overflow if the control loop itself fails.
Advanced Explain why a loop diagram exists separately from the P&ID

The P&ID already shows that LT-101 connects to LIC-101. Why does a separate loop diagram exist for the same loop, rather than just adding more detail directly onto the P&ID?

Answer A P&ID is deliberately scoped to show the whole process at a consistent level of detail across potentially hundreds of loops — adding full terminal-by-terminal wiring detail for every loop would make it unreadable at the level it's meant to serve (understanding the overall process). A loop diagram trades that breadth for depth on exactly one loop, giving a technician the terminal numbers, wire numbers, and I/O card/channel information actually needed to troubleshoot that specific loop, the same way the Control Panel Design tutorial's as-built wiring detail exists separately from a simpler overview schematic.
