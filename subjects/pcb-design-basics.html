From Breadboard to PCB

Layout

A breadboard prototype — the natural output of the Arduino and ESP32 tutorials' worked examples — is fast to build and change, but fragile: loose connections, parasitic capacitance from the long jumper wires, and nothing holding it together if the project gets physically moved or bumped. A PCB fixes exact component positions and connections permanently (or at least until deliberately reworked), removes the breadboard's own electrical imperfections, and is what turns a one-off prototype into something reproducible — ten identical boards are no harder to order than one.

Schematic Capture Basics

Process

A schematic is a symbolic, not physical, representation of a circuit — components drawn as standard symbols, connected by wires representing electrical nets (every point on a net is electrically the same node). Labeled nets (naming a wire +5V or MODBUS_TX rather than drawing it crossing the whole sheet) keep complex schematics readable; larger designs split into hierarchical sheets, one per functional block (power supply, microcontroller, I/O), echoing the same "break it into manageable pieces" principle as the PLC Learning tutorial's program organization section.

Through-Hole vs Surface Mount

Layout

Mountingleads through drilled holes, soldered on the opposite sidepads on the same surface as the component

Most real boards mix both: SMD for the bulk of small logic/passive components, THT for connectors and anything needing real mechanical strength (board edge connectors, large electrolytic capacitors, anything that gets physically stressed in use).

Footprints & the Schematic Link

Layout

A footprint is the physical land pattern — pad shapes, sizes, and spacing — matching a specific component package, linked to that component's schematic symbol. The link matters enormously: a schematic symbol assigned the wrong footprint (a resistor's symbol linked to a connector's footprint, or simply the wrong package size) produces a board that's electrically correct on paper but physically impossible to assemble, usually not caught until the boards and parts have both already arrived.

Choosing Design Software

Process

KiCad is the standard free, open-source starting point — a complete schematic capture, layout, and 3D-preview toolchain with no cost barrier and a large library of community footprints, capable of professional-grade boards well beyond hobbyist scale. Commercial alternatives (Altium Designer, Eagle, and others) add polish and some workflow conveniences at real licensing cost, generally worth it once a team or production volume justifies it rather than for a first board.

Copper Weight & Trace Thickness

Layout

PCB copper is specified by weight per unit area rather than a direct thickness measurement — a holdover from how copper foil is actually manufactured and sold. 1oz copper (1.37 mils / 35µm) is the default for the overwhelming majority of designs; 2oz doubles the thickness, used where higher current or lower trace resistance is needed without impractically wide traces. Both the trace-width and trace-resistance calculators above take copper weight as a direct input, since thickness is exactly what determines a trace's cross-sectional area for a given width.

Sizing Traces for Current: IPC-2221

Layout

A trace that's too narrow for its current overheats — potentially enough to damage the board's substrate or the trace itself. The IPC-2221 standard provides an empirical formula (not a simple linear rule) relating current, allowed temperature rise, and required cross-sectional area:

Area (mils²) = (I / (k × ΔT^0.44))^(1/0.725)
Width (mils) = Area / thickness

Worked example: 1A, 10°C allowed rise, external trace, 1oz copper → Area ≈ 16.3 mils², giving a width of about 11.9 mils (≈0.3mm). The same current and temperature rise on an internal trace needs roughly 31 mils — about 2.6× the area — because a buried trace has no direct air cooling and must shed its heat entirely through the surrounding board material. Confirm both in the calculator above.

This is a thermal limit, not a safety margin IPC-2221 sizes traces to control self-heating, not to provide headroom against fault conditions the way a circuit breaker does. For anything carrying meaningful current in a safety-relevant application, add real margin beyond the bare minimum this formula returns.

Trace Resistance & Voltage Drop

Layout

Beyond simply not overheating, a long or narrow trace carrying real current also drops real voltage — the same concern as the Control Panel Design tutorial's wire voltage-drop section, just at PCB scale with a rectangular rather than round conductor: R = ρ × L / (W × T). Worked example: a 50-mil-wide, 100mm-long, 1oz trace has a resistance of about 0.038Ω; at 2A, that's a 0.076V drop — small in absolute terms, but worth checking explicitly for long power-supply traces feeding sensitive analog circuitry, where even tens of millivolts can matter.

Vias: Connecting Layers

Layout

A via is a plated hole connecting copper on different layers — the layer-to-layer equivalent of a wire jumper. Standard through-hole vias run the full board thickness; more advanced (and more expensive) techniques include blind vias (connecting an outer layer to an inner one, not all the way through) and buried vias (connecting only inner layers, invisible from either surface) — both used to pack denser routing on complex multi-layer boards, well beyond what a first design typically needs.

Design Rule Check (DRC)

Process

Before any design is sent to fabrication, DRC automatically checks the layout against a set of manufacturing and electrical constraints: minimum trace width and spacing (narrower than the fab can reliably produce means broken or shorted traces), minimum via/hole sizes, clearance from the board edge, and that every net in the schematic actually has a corresponding, fully-connected copper path (an unrouted or partially routed net is exactly the kind of mistake DRC exists to catch before it becomes an expensive, unusable board). Running DRC clean is a hard gate before ordering, not an optional nicety.

Power Integrity: Decoupling & Planes

Layout

Every IC's power pin needs a nearby decoupling capacitor — the same 100nF-per-pin pattern from the Electronics & Instrumentation tutorial's capacitor section — placed as physically close to the pin as possible, so the capacitor (not a long trace back to the power supply) is what responds to the IC's fast, momentary current spikes. A solid ground plane (see Layer Stackup above) is the other half of this: it gives those return currents, and the decoupling capacitor's own loop, the shortest possible path, which directly reduces both noise and radiated EMI — the same underlying physics as the Control Panel Design tutorial's cable-segregation advice, just at board scale instead of panel scale.

Signal Integrity: Return Paths

Layout

A signal doesn't just travel down its trace — the return current travels back through whatever's nearest and lowest-impedance, ideally the solid ground plane directly underneath it. Route a trace across a gap or split in that ground plane, and the return current is forced to detour around the gap, creating a larger current loop that radiates more EMI and picks up more noise — a subtle, easy-to-introduce layout mistake with real consequences, especially for the fast-switching signals this Handbook's VFD and ESP32 tutorials already flag as noise sources. For truly high-speed or critical signals, controlled impedance routing (precise trace width/spacing/plane-distance combinations, calculated to hit a target impedance like 50Ω or 100Ω differential) goes a step further — a specialist topic in its own right, built entirely on this same return-path foundation.

Thermal Design on a PCB

Layout

A copper pour (a large filled area of copper, often tied to ground) doubles as a basic heat spreader for a moderately hot component — a small regulator or driver IC — pulling heat away from the component into a larger thermal mass. Thermal vias (a cluster of vias under a hot component's thermal pad) carry that heat through to a copper pour on another layer, or to the opposite side of the board where airflow or a heatsink can remove it more effectively. For genuinely high-power components, this still only goes so far — the VFD tutorial's enclosure-level cooling options (fans, heat exchangers) exist precisely because board-level copper alone has real thermal limits.

Design for Manufacturing & Assembly

Process

DFM (manufacturability) and DFA (assembly) are the practical constraints a fab house and assembly house actually work within: minimum trace/space the fab's process can reliably hit, enough clearance around components for automated pick-and-place equipment, fiducial markers (reference marks the assembly machine's camera uses to align the board precisely), and panelization (multiple board copies arranged on one larger panel for efficient fabrication and assembly, then separated afterward). Designing with these constraints in mind from the start avoids a board that's electrically perfect but expensive or impossible to actually build at scale.

The schematic places the ESP32 module's footprint, a level-shifted GPIO output (the Raspberry Pi tutorial's 5V→3.3V concern runs the other direction here — the relay's drive transistor needs to accept the ESP32's 3.3V logic), a flyback diode across the relay coil, and the same 100nF decoupling capacitor pattern on the ESP32's power pins. ● Practice Exercises
Basic Convert copper weight to thickness

What's the thickness, in mils and millimetres, of 2oz copper?

Answer 2 × 1.37 = 2.74 mils, which is 2.74 × 0.0254 ≈ 0.0696mm (≈70µm).
Basic Choose THT or SMD for a connector

A board needs a USB connector that will be plugged and unplugged frequently in the field. Should it be a through-hole or surface-mount part, and why?

Answer Through-hole (or at minimum, an SMD part with additional through-hole mechanical anchor pins, common on connector-specific footprints). Repeated plug/unplug cycles put real mechanical stress on the joint, and through-hole soldering is substantially stronger than a surface-mount pad alone.
Intermediate Size a trace using IPC-2221

An external, 1oz copper trace needs to carry 1A with no more than 10°C temperature rise. What width does IPC-2221 call for?

Answer Area = (1/(0.048×10^0.44))^(1/0.725) ≈ 16.3 mils²; dividing by the 1.37-mil thickness gives a width of about 11.9 mils (≈0.3mm). Confirm it in the calculator above.
Intermediate Compute a trace's voltage drop

A 50-mil-wide, 1oz, 100mm-long trace carries 2A. What's its resistance and voltage drop?

Answer R ≈ 0.038Ω; at 2A, Vdrop = 2 × 0.038 ≈ 0.076V. Small in absolute terms, but worth checking for long analog power traces where tens of millivolts matter.
Advanced Explain why internal traces need to be wider

For the same current and allowed temperature rise, an internal (buried) trace needs roughly 2.6× the cross-sectional area of an external trace. Why?

Answer An external trace is in direct contact with air, which helps carry heat away by convection. An internal trace is fully buried in solid dielectric material with no direct air cooling, so it must dissipate the same heat entirely through conduction into the surrounding board material — a less effective cooling path, reflected in IPC-2221's halved k constant (0.024 vs 0.048) for internal traces.
Advanced Diagnose a ground-plane routing mistake

A 4-layer board has a solid ground plane on layer 2, but a fast digital signal trace on layer 1 is routed directly over a slot cut into that ground plane (for an unrelated mechanical reason). What problem does this create, and why?

Answer The signal's return current can no longer travel in a short, direct path on the plane immediately beneath the trace — it's forced to detour around the slot, creating a much larger current loop. That larger loop radiates more EMI and is more susceptible to picking up external noise, exactly the signal-integrity problem a solid ground plane is meant to prevent. The fix is routing the mechanical slot (or the trace) so the two never overlap, or adding a stitching via to give the return current a path across the gap.
