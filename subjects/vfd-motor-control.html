● Basic

Why Motors Need Starting Control

Motor

Switch a standard induction motor straight onto full line voltage and it draws 5–7 times its rated running current for the first second or two, until it comes up to speed — inrush current. For a small motor on a stiff supply this is a non-issue; for a large motor, it can sag the whole site's voltage, trip upstream protection, and deliver a mechanical jolt to whatever the motor drives (belts, gearboxes, couplings) every single start. Starting methods exist to manage this inrush, this jolt, or both.

3-Phase Induction Motor Basics

Motor

Three AC supply phases, 120° apart in time, energize windings arranged 120° apart around the stator — the combination produces a magnetic field that rotates at a speed set purely by supply frequency and how many poles the motor is wound for:

N_sync = 120 × f / P

Worked example: 50Hz supply, 4-pole motor → 120×50/4 = 1500 RPM. On a 60Hz supply the same 4-pole motor spins at 120×60/4 = 1800 RPM — this is exactly why motors rated for 50Hz regions and 60Hz regions carry different nameplate speeds even when otherwise identical.

The rotor itself never quite catches up to this rotating field — it needs that relative motion (the field "cutting across" the rotor) to induce the current that produces torque in the first place. The resulting speed deficit is slip, typically 2–5% at full load: a 1500 RPM sync-speed motor might show 1450 RPM on its nameplate.

Contactors & Direct-On-Line Starting

Motor

A contactor is a heavy-duty, coil-operated switch sized to make and break motor-starting current repeatedly without welding its contacts shut. Direct-On-Line (DOL) starting is simply closing one contactor straight across the supply to the motor — the simplest, cheapest method, and the default choice whenever the motor is small enough, or the supply stiff enough, that the inrush current isn't a problem.

An overload relay in series with the contactor monitors running current and trips the contactor open if it stays above a set threshold for too long — the motor's primary protection against a stalled rotor, a mechanical jam, or a developing winding fault, all of which show up first as excess current.

Star-Delta Starting

Motor

A 3-phase motor's windings can be connected two ways: delta (each winding across two line phases, full line voltage) for normal running, or star/wye (each winding from a line phase to a common neutral point, only 1/√3 of line voltage per winding) for starting. Lower winding voltage means lower winding current, and — because of how star and delta relate line current to winding current — the line current the supply actually sees drops to almost exactly a third of what direct-on-line would draw.

Worked example: a motor with 100A FLA and a typical 6× DOL inrush multiplier draws 600A starting DOL, versus roughly 200A starting star-delta. Try other values in the comparator tool above.

Soft Starters

Motor

A solid-state soft starter uses back-to-back thyristors to ramp the applied voltage up smoothly over an adjustable time, rather than switching abruptly between star and delta taps. This gives a smoother mechanical start (no torque step at the star-to-delta transition point) and more flexible tuning of the start profile, at higher cost than star-delta and without the further benefit of variable running speed that a full VFD provides.

Choosing a starting method DOL: simple loads, small motors, stiff supply. Star-delta: larger motors, light-starting-torque loads (pumps/fans), cost-sensitive. Soft starter: smoother start needed, but running speed stays fixed. VFD: variable running speed is needed, or the energy savings from reduced-speed running (see Affinity Laws below) justify the cost.

What Is a VFD

Drive

A Variable Frequency Drive doesn't just reduce starting current — it changes the motor's running speed continuously, by generating a supply at whatever frequency the speed reference calls for. Since synchronous speed is directly set by frequency (N_sync = 120f/P), controlling frequency controls speed directly.

V/Hz Control

Drive

A motor's magnetic flux depends on the ratio of applied voltage to frequency. Drop frequency without also dropping voltage and the motor saturates magnetically, overheating and drawing excess current; a VFD's basic control law holds V/Hz constant up to the motor's rated (nameplate) frequency, keeping available torque roughly constant across that whole range — the "constant torque region."

V/Hz ratio = V_rated / f_rated
V_target  = (V/Hz ratio) × f_target

Worked example: a motor rated 415V/50Hz has a ratio of 415/50 = 8.3 V/Hz. Commanded to run at 37.5Hz (75% speed), the drive outputs 8.3 × 37.5 = 311.3V — also 75% of rated voltage, keeping the ratio, and the flux, constant. Above rated frequency, voltage can't keep rising (the drive is already at its DC bus limit), so flux — and available torque — falls off; this is the "constant power" region used for light loads that need to run above nameplate speed.

Drive
MethodHow it works

VFD Wiring & Cable Effects

Drive

The PWM output between drive and motor switches extremely fast, which makes the motor cable behave less like a simple wire and more like a transmission line at longer lengths:

  • Use shielded, VFD-rated motor cable, with the shield grounded at the drive end, to contain the radiated EMI those fast edges produce.
  • Keep motor cable runs within the drive manufacturer's rated length for the installation (often tens of metres without mitigation) — beyond that, voltage reflections at the motor end can roughly double the peak voltage the motor insulation actually sees, a mechanism covered in Bearing Currents & Reflected Wave below.
  • Route motor cables separately from signal/control cables, crossing at 90° where they must cross, to minimise coupled noise into sensitive instrumentation wiring — the same grounding/shielding discipline as the Electronics & Instrumentation tutorial's signal conditioning section, applied to a much higher-energy source.

Motor Protection

Motor

Beyond a basic thermal overload relay (which models heating from current alone), VFD-driven motors add protection layers: PTC thermistors embedded directly in the windings feed a true winding-temperature signal back to the drive, catching faults a current-based model would miss (e.g. reduced cooling at low speed, since a self-cooled motor's fan slows down with it). Drives also typically offer configurable electronic overload curves matched to the specific motor's thermal characteristics, often more accurate than a generic bimetallic relay.

Harmonics & Power Quality

Drive

A VFD's rectifier doesn't draw a clean sine-wave current from the supply — it draws current in pulses near each voltage peak, rich in harmonic frequencies (multiples of the fundamental 50/60Hz). On a site with many drives, these harmonics distort the shared supply voltage waveform for every other connected load, can overheat transformers and neutral conductors, and may trip power-quality limits imposed by the utility.

Mitigation scales with severity: a simple line reactor (an inductor in series with the drive's input) smooths the current pulses significantly at low cost; dedicated harmonic filters or multi-pulse rectifier designs go further where regulatory limits or sensitive nearby equipment demand it.

Bearing Currents & Reflected Wave

Drive

The same fast PWM switching that enables variable frequency output has two field-notorious side effects:

  • Reflected wave: on a motor cable long enough relative to the switching edge speed, voltage pulses reflect off the impedance mismatch at the motor terminals, momentarily doubling peak voltage there — over time this can puncture motor winding insulation not rated for it. Output reactors or dV/dt filters at the drive slow the edges enough to prevent this.
  • Shaft (bearing) currents: the fast switching induces a small common-mode voltage on the motor shaft; without a return path, it discharges through the bearings' lubricating film as tiny electrical discharge machining (EDM) pits, slowly destroying the bearing races over months. Shaft grounding rings (brushes that give the induced current a low-impedance path to ground, bypassing the bearing) and common-mode chokes on the output are the standard fixes.
Both failure modes are slow and easy to misdiagnose A bearing fluted by EDM currents or insulation weakened by reflected-wave stress can run for months before failing, long after the VFD was commissioned — making it easy to blame "bad bearings" or "bad motor" rather than the actual cable-length or filtering gap that caused it.

Energy Savings: the Affinity Laws

Motor

For centrifugal pumps and fans specifically (not all loads — this doesn't apply to constant-torque loads like conveyors or positive-displacement pumps), three relationships hold:

Flow     ∝ speed
Pressure ∝ speed²
Power    ∝ speed³

The cubic power relationship is the headline number: run a pump at 80% speed and it still delivers 80% flow, but draws only 0.8³ = 51.2% of rated power. At 50% speed, flow is halved but power drops to just 12.5%. This is why VFD-controlled pumps and fans so often pay for themselves quickly on sites that previously throttled flow with a valve or damper while the motor kept running at full speed (and full power) regardless.

Worked example: a 55kW rated pump running at 70% speed draws 55 × 0.7³ = 55 × 0.343 ≈ 18.9kW — roughly a third of its full-speed power, for 70% of the flow. Try other speeds in the calculator above.

Integrating VFDs with PLCs

Drive

A PLC commands a VFD's speed one of two ways, both already covered elsewhere in this Handbook: an analog output — typically 4–20mA or 0–10V — scaled to the drive's configured frequency range (exactly the Analog I/O Basics and scaling math from the PLC Learning tutorial), or a digital fieldbus connection, very often Modbus RTU/TCP, writing a speed-reference register and reading back status/fault words (see the Modbus tutorial's register addressing and function code sections).

Worked example: a drive's analog speed reference spans 0–50Hz over its 4–20mA input. To command 37.5Hz (75% speed), the PLC's analog output must produce 4 + (37.5−0)/(50−0) × 16 = 4 + 12 = 16mA — the same 4–20mA scaling formula from the Electronics & Instrumentation tutorial, just driving a drive's reference input instead of reading a transmitter.

● Practice Exercises
Basic Find synchronous speed and slip

A 4-pole motor runs from a 50Hz supply and shows 1450 RPM on its nameplate at full load. What's the synchronous speed, and what's the slip as a percentage?

Answer N_sync = 120×50/4 = 1500 RPM. Slip = (1500−1450)/1500 = 0.0333, i.e. 3.33%.
Basic Compare DOL and star-delta starting current

A motor has an FLA of 100A and a typical direct-on-line inrush multiplier of 6×. What's the DOL starting current, and roughly what would star-delta starting draw instead?

Answer DOL: 100 × 6 = 600A. Star-delta cuts line starting current to roughly a third: 600/3 = 200A.
Intermediate Compute a VFD's output voltage at reduced frequency

A motor is rated 415V at 50Hz. Running it at 37.5Hz under constant V/Hz control, what output voltage should the drive produce?

Answer Ratio = 415/50 = 8.3 V/Hz. At 37.5Hz: 8.3 × 37.5 = 311.3V — also 75% of rated voltage, matching the 75% frequency, so flux (and available torque) stays constant.
Intermediate Diagnose a DC bus overvoltage fault

A VFD trips on a DC bus overvoltage fault every time an operator commands a fast stop on a large, high-inertia fan. What's the likely cause, and what are two ways to fix it?

Answer The fan's inertia keeps it spinning faster than the commanded decel ramp allows, so the motor regenerates energy into the DC bus faster than the drive can dissipate it, pushing bus voltage up until it trips. Fixes: lengthen the decel ramp time so the load can slow down more gradually, or add a braking resistor (dynamic braking) to actively dissipate the regenerated energy as heat.
Advanced Quantify pump energy savings at reduced speed

A pump rated 55kW at full speed is instead run at 70% speed via VFD to match a reduced process demand. Using the affinity laws, roughly how much power does it draw?

Answer Power ∝ speed³: 55 × 0.7³ = 55 × 0.343 ≈ 18.9kW — about a third of full-speed power, for 70% of the flow. Confirm it in the Affinity Laws calculator above.
Advanced Scale a PLC analog output to a VFD speed reference

A VFD accepts a 4–20mA analog speed reference mapped to 0–50Hz. A PLC needs to command 37.5Hz (75% of max speed). What current should its analog output produce?

Answer mA = 4 + (37.5−0)/(50−0) × 16 = 4 + 0.75×16 = 4 + 12 = 16mA.
