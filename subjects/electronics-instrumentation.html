● Basic

Charge, Voltage & Current

Electronics

Everything here comes down to three quantities. Charge is the stuff electrons carry. Voltage (volts, V) is the push on that charge — the electrical pressure difference between two points, exactly the way a water tower's height creates water pressure. Current (amps, A) is how much charge actually flows per second — the water moving through a pipe.

Conventional current is drawn flowing from + to −, even though the electrons physically doing the moving travel the other way. Every schematic, every formula in this tutorial, uses the conventional direction — it's a 1700s convention (Benjamin Franklin guessed the sign of charge before anyone knew what an electron was) that stuck.

Field intuition Voltage exists between two points; it has no meaning at a single point without a reference. "5V" always means 5V relative to something — usually a ground or 0V rail. Current, by contrast, flows through a single point in a circuit.

Ohm's Law

Electronics

The single most-used equation in electronics: V = I × R. Voltage across a resistor equals the current through it times its resistance (ohms, Ω). Rearranged, it answers the three questions you'll ask constantly:

KnownSolve forFormula
I, RVV = I × R
V, RII = V / R
V, IRR = V / I

Worked example: an LED needs 20mA (0.02A) and drops 2V across itself. Powered from a 9V supply, the resistor in series must drop the remaining 9 − 2 = 7V at 0.02A: R = 7 / 0.02 = 350Ω. This exact problem is Exercise 1 below — try the calculator above to confirm it.

Series & Parallel Circuits

Electronics

How resistors combine depends entirely on whether current has one path or several:

SeriesParallel
Total resistanceR1 + R2 + …1 / (1/R1 + 1/R2 + …)
Two-resistor shortcut—(R1×R2) / (R1+R2)
Currentsame through allsplits, more through the lower-R branch
Voltagesplits, more across the higher-R partsame across all branches

Worked example: 100Ω and 200Ω in parallel: (100×200)/(100+200) = 66.7Ω — always less than the smallest resistor, because adding a path can only make it easier for current to flow.

Power & Energy

Electronics

Power (watts, W) is the rate energy is used: P = V × I. Combined with Ohm's Law, this gives two more forms worth memorising, because you rarely have all three of V, I, R measured at once:

P = V × I
P = I² × R      (useful when you know the current through a resistor)
P = V² / R      (useful when you know the voltage across it)

Worked example: a 220Ω resistor with 5V across it dissipates 5²/220 = 0.114W — well inside a standard ¼W (0.25W) resistor's rating. Exceed a resistor's power rating and it overheats, drifts in value, and eventually fails open or scorches the board — always check P = V²/R against the component's rated wattage, not just whether the circuit "looks fine."

Energy vs. power Power is instantaneous (watts); energy is power over time (watt-hours, or joules = watts × seconds). A phone charger rated 10W delivers 10 watt-hours of energy per hour it runs — this is the number on your electricity bill, not the instantaneous power alone.

Capacitors

Electronics

A capacitor stores charge on two plates separated by an insulator, resisting changes in voltage across it. Capacitance is measured in farads (F) — in practice always microfarads (µF, 10−&sup6;), nanofarads (nF, 10−&sup9;), or picofarads (pF, 10−¹²), since a full farad is enormous.

Charging through a resistor follows an exponential curve characterised by the time constant τ = RC (seconds): after one τ, the capacitor reaches ~63% of the final voltage; after 5τ, it's considered fully charged (~99.3%). This single relationship underlies timing circuits, debounce filters, and the RC low-pass/high-pass filters covered later.

Where you'll meet them Decoupling/bypass (a 100nF cap next to every IC's power pin, absorbing fast current spikes), bulk smoothing after a rectifier, timing, and as the frequency-selective half of every RC filter.

Inductors

Electronics

An inductor is a coil that resists changes in current, storing energy in a magnetic field rather than an electric field. It's the mirror image of a capacitor: where a cap fights a sudden voltage change, a coil fights a sudden current change — which is exactly why switching off an inductive load (a relay coil, a motor) without a flyback diode produces a damaging voltage spike as the collapsing field tries to force current to keep flowing.

Measured in henries (H), almost always millihenries (mH) or microhenries (µH) in practice. You'll meet inductors far less often than resistors and capacitors in general electronics, but they're central to power-supply switching regulators, motor/relay flyback protection, and RF work.

Multimeter & Breadboard Basics

Electronics

Multimeter: to measure voltage, probes go across (in parallel with) the two points — the meter has high internal resistance so it barely disturbs the circuit. To measure current, the meter must go in series, breaking the circuit open so all the current flows through it — its internal resistance is deliberately tiny. Mixing these up (measuring current in voltage mode, or voltage in current mode across a supply) is the single most common way to blow a multimeter's fuse, or worse, short a supply.

Breadboard: the five holes in each short vertical column are internally connected — that's one node. The long rows top and bottom (marked + and −) run the full length of the board and are meant for power rails. A component straddling the centre gap connects to two separate nodes, which is exactly how ICs get placed: pins on one side land in a different node from pins on the other.

Before powering up Trace your circuit once with a finger, following current from supply+ through every component back to supply−, before switching anything on. Most beginner smoke comes from a reversed polarity component or a direct short between the rails, not a subtle design flaw.

Kirchhoff's Laws

Electronics

Two bookkeeping rules that let you analyse any circuit, not just simple series/parallel combinations:

  • Current Law (KCL): the current flowing into a node equals the current flowing out. Charge doesn't pile up or vanish at a junction.
  • Voltage Law (KVL): the sum of voltage drops around any closed loop equals zero. Whatever voltage the source supplies, the components in the loop must account for all of it.

These two laws are why the series and parallel formulas work in the first place — they're the general case; series/parallel are the easy special cases.

AC Fundamentals

Electronics

Alternating current reverses direction periodically, typically as a sine wave, described by frequency (Hz, cycles per second) and amplitude. Because a sine wave's instantaneous value is constantly changing, "the voltage" of an AC supply is usually quoted as RMS (root-mean-square) — the DC-equivalent value that would deliver the same average power into a resistive load.

V_RMS = V_peak / √2   (≈ V_peak × 0.707)

Worked example: mains power's peak voltage is about 325V; 325 / √2 ≈ 230V — the "230V" on your wall socket is already an RMS figure, not the peak.

Impedance & Reactance

Electronics

Resistance opposes current the same way at any frequency. Capacitors and inductors don't — their opposition (reactance, X, in ohms) depends on frequency:

X_C = 1 / (2πfC)     — capacitive reactance falls as frequency rises
X_L = 2πfL          — inductive reactance rises as frequency rises

Impedance (Z) is the combined opposition of resistance and reactance together, and unlike plain resistance it also shifts the phase between voltage and current. This frequency-dependence is the entire reason RC and RL networks can act as filters: at DC a capacitor looks like an open circuit (XC→∞), at very high frequency it looks like a short (XC→0).

Diodes & Rectification

Electronics

A diode conducts current in one direction only, dropping a roughly constant forward voltage while doing so (~0.7V for silicon, ~0.3V for germanium/Schottky, ~1.8–3.3V for visible LEDs depending on colour). Below that forward voltage, and in the reverse direction entirely, it blocks current (until a much higher reverse breakdown voltage, which normal use avoids).

Arrange four diodes as a bridge rectifier and an AC input — which reverses polarity every half-cycle — comes out the other side always flowing the same direction, the first step in turning AC mains into usable DC. A smoothing capacitor across the output then fills in the gaps between pulses.

Transistors as Switches

Electronics

A bipolar transistor (BJT) has three legs — base, collector, emitter — and a simple rule for switch-mode use: a small current into the base allows a much larger current to flow collector-to-emitter. That current ratio is the gain, hFE (often 50–300). This is how a microcontroller pin, which might only safely source a few mA, can switch an amp-scale relay or motor on and off: the small base current, through a current-limiting resistor, turns on a much bigger collector current.

MOSFETs do the same job but are controlled by gate voltage rather than base current, and dominate modern switching applications (motor drivers, power supplies) because they waste far less energy as heat when fully on.

Digital Logic Basics

Electronics

Digital circuits treat voltage as one of two states — HIGH (logic 1) and LOW (logic 0) — and combine them with logic gates:

GateOutput is HIGH when…
ANDall inputs are HIGH
ORany input is HIGH
NOTthe single input is LOW (it inverts)
NANDNOT(all inputs HIGH) — the inverse of AND
XORexactly one input is HIGH (not both, not neither)

Every digital system — a microcontroller's internal logic, a PLC's ladder logic (see the companion Modbus tutorial), an ADC's successive-approximation register — is built from these few primitives combined at scale.

RC Filters

Electronics

A resistor and capacitor in series, with the output taken across one of them, forms the simplest filter:

  • Low-pass (output across C): passes low frequencies, attenuates high ones. Capacitive reactance is high at low frequency (blocks little, drop stays small), low at high frequency (shorts the output to ground).
  • High-pass (output across R): the opposite — passes high frequencies, attenuates low ones.

Both share the same cutoff frequency formula: fc = 1 / (2πRC), the point where output power has dropped to half (−3dB). Worked example: R = 4.7kΩ, C = 10nF → fc = 1/(2π × 4700 × 10×10−&sup9;) ≈ 3386Hz — verify it in the calculator above.

Operational Amplifiers

Electronics

An op-amp is a high-gain differential amplifier, almost always used with external resistors that set a precise, predictable gain via negative feedback. Two configurations cover most practical use:

ConfigurationGainNotes
Non-invertingAv = 1 + Rf/Rinoutput in phase with input; gain always ≥1
InvertingAv = −Rf/Rinoutput 180° out of phase; gain can be <1

Worked examples: non-inverting with Rf = 100kΩ, Rin = 10kΩ gives 1 + 10 = 11× gain. Inverting with Rf = 47kΩ, Rin = 4.7kΩ gives −10× gain. This is precisely the building block instrumentation uses to amplify a sensor's millivolt-level output up to a usable range before it ever reaches an ADC — see Signal Conditioning below.

Power Supply Design

Electronics

A typical linear DC supply chain: transformer (steps mains voltage down) → bridge rectifier (four diodes, converts AC to pulsating DC) → smoothing capacitor (fills the gaps between pulses) → linear regulator (holds the output at a fixed voltage regardless of load, by dropping the excess as heat).

Linear regulators are simple and quiet (low electrical noise) but wasteful — the voltage they drop times the current is dissipated as heat. Switching regulators (buck/boost converters) achieve the same job far more efficiently by rapidly switching an inductor on and off, at the cost of some electrical noise, which matters when the supply feeds sensitive analog instrumentation.

Transducer Characteristics

Instrumentation

A sensor's datasheet is only useful once these terms stop being jargon:

TermMeaning
Accuracyhow close a reading is to the true value
Precisionhow repeatable readings are, regardless of whether they're accurate (a scale that's always wrong by the exact same 2kg is precise but not accurate)
Sensitivityoutput change per unit input change (e.g. mV/°C) — the slope of the transfer function
Linearityhow closely the real transfer function matches a straight line across its range
Hysteresisoutput differs depending on whether the input is rising or falling through the same value
Resolutionthe smallest change the sensor (or its ADC) can distinguish — separate from accuracy
Repeatabilityagreement between successive measurements of the same quantity, same conditions
Accuracy ≠ precision ≠ resolution A 24-bit ADC has enormous resolution, but if the sensor feeding it is only accurate to ±2%, those extra bits are measuring noise, not reality. Always match conditioning and digitisation precision to what the sensor can actually deliver.

Thermocouples & RTDs

Instrumentation

The two workhorse temperature sensors work on opposite principles:

  • Thermocouple: two dissimilar metal wires joined at the measurement point generate a small voltage (microvolts per °C) proportional to the temperature difference between that junction and a reference junction — the Seebeck effect. Cheap, rugged, wide range (Type K: −200°C to +1260°C), but needs cold-junction compensation: since the voltage depends on the difference between two junctions, the instrument must separately measure the temperature at its own terminals (the "cold junction") and add that back in, or every reading is off by the ambient temperature at the connector.
  • RTD (Resistance Temperature Detector): a precision resistor (commonly platinum, "Pt100" = 100Ω at 0°C) whose resistance increases predictably and near-linearly with temperature. More accurate and stable than a thermocouple, narrower range, needs a small excitation current run through it (which itself generates a little self-heating error) and its resistance change read out — often via a bridge circuit.

Strain Gauges & the Wheatstone Bridge

Instrumentation

A strain gauge is a thin conductive foil whose resistance changes very slightly as it's stretched or compressed with whatever it's bonded to — the basis of most load cells and pressure sensors. The problem: that resistance change is tiny (fractions of a percent), far too small to read directly against a resistor's normal manufacturing tolerance. The Wheatstone bridge solves this by measuring the imbalance rather than the absolute value:

For a bridge with one active arm, the approximate output is Vout ≈ (Vex/4) × (ΔR/R). Worked example: Vex = 5V, ΔR/R = 0.002 (0.2% change) → Vout = 2.5mV — a signal small enough that it almost always needs an instrumentation amplifier (a precision differential op-amp stage) before anything downstream can use it.

Pressure, Flow & Level Sensing

Instrumentation
QuantityCommon sensing principle
Pressurea diaphragm deflects under pressure; strain gauges (often in a bridge, as above) or a piezoresistive element convert that deflection to an electrical signal
Flowdifferential pressure across a known restriction (orifice plate), a spinning turbine's rotation rate, or ultrasonic/Doppler time-of-flight through the fluid
Levelhydrostatic pressure at the tank bottom, a float switch, ultrasonic/radar time-of-flight from the top, or capacitance change between a probe and the tank wall

Notice the pattern: almost none of these sense the quantity you actually want directly. Flow is usually inferred from a pressure difference; level is usually inferred from pressure or time-of-flight. Knowing what's really being measured is often the key to diagnosing why a reading looks wrong (e.g. a "level" sensor reading hydrostatic pressure will mis-read if the fluid's density changes).

The 4–20mA Current Loop

Instrumentation

Industrial instruments overwhelmingly transmit their reading as a current, not a voltage, over a single twisted pair — and the range starts at 4mA, not 0mA. Both choices solve real field problems:

  • Current, not voltage: current is the same at every point in a series loop regardless of wire resistance or length, so voltage drop along a long cable run doesn't corrupt the reading the way it would with a voltage signal.
  • 4mA as "zero," not 0mA: a dead loop (wire cut, transmitter powered off) reads 0mA, which is clearly distinguishable from a live "zero" reading at 4mA — the gap between 0 and 4mA is a built-in fault indicator, often called "live zero."

Worked example: a temperature transmitter calibrated 0–100°C reads 12mA. That's the midpoint of the 4–20mA span, so value = 0 + (12−4)/16 × 100 = 50.0°C. For a transmitter calibrated to a range with an offset, e.g. −20 to 80°C at 14mA: −20 + (14−4)/16 × 100 = 42.5°C. Try both in the converter tool above.

Analog-to-Digital Conversion

Instrumentation

An ADC converts a continuous voltage into a discrete number, with two limits that together set how faithfully it represents the original signal:

  • Resolution (bits, n): the number of discrete steps is 2n. The size of each step — the smallest voltage change the ADC can distinguish — is Vref / 2n.
  • Sampling rate: how often the ADC takes a reading. The Nyquist criterion requires sampling at more than twice the highest frequency present in the signal, or higher-frequency content folds back and masquerades as a lower frequency ("aliasing") — which is why anti-aliasing filters (another RC low-pass, often) sit in front of an ADC's input.

Worked examples: a 10-bit ADC with a 5V reference has a step size of 5/1024 ≈ 4.88mV — any voltage change smaller than that is invisible to it. A 12-bit ADC at 3.3V does better: 3.3/4096 ≈ 0.806mV per step.

Resolution isn't accuracy A fine step size only matters if the signal feeding the ADC is clean and the reference voltage is stable — a noisy or poorly conditioned input will have its noise faithfully digitised right along with the real signal, at whatever resolution you've paid for.

Signal Conditioning & Noise

Instrumentation

Between a sensor's raw output and a usable digital reading sits a chain of conditioning, typically some mix of:

  • Amplification — a millivolt-level bridge or thermocouple signal needs gain (an instrumentation amplifier, built around op-amps) before an ADC can resolve it meaningfully.
  • Filtering — an RC low-pass ahead of the ADC removes high-frequency noise and prevents aliasing.
  • Isolation — opto-isolators or isolation amplifiers break a direct electrical connection between field wiring and control electronics, protecting against ground potential differences and fault voltages (note the opto-isolated inputs on the relay board covered in the earlier field guide — this is exactly why they're there).

Grounding and noise: a ground loop forms when two points that should be at the same potential (both called "ground") actually differ slightly, driving a stray current through whatever signal cable connects them — a classic source of hum and drift in long sensor runs. Two standard defenses: run sensor signals differentially (two wires carrying the signal and its exact inverse, so noise picked up equally by both wires cancels at the receiver) rather than single-ended against a shared ground, and tie cable shields to ground at one end only, so the shield can't itself become part of a ground loop.

Closed-Loop Control & PID

Instrumentation

An open-loop system acts without checking the result (a timer-based sprinkler that runs for 10 minutes regardless of whether the soil is already wet). A closed-loop system measures its own output and feeds that measurement back to correct itself — a thermostat is the everyday example. The measurement chain covered above is exactly the "sense" half of that loop.

The standard closed-loop controller is PID — Proportional, Integral, Derivative — which computes a correction from the error (setpoint minus measured value) three ways at once:

TermReacts toEffect
Pthe current errorbigger error → bigger correction, but settles with some steady-state error left over
Iaccumulated past erroreliminates that leftover steady-state error over time, but can overshoot if too aggressive
Dhow fast the error is changingdampens overshoot by reacting to the trend, not just the current value

Tuning the three gains (Kp, Ki, Kd) that weight each term is its own deep field — the summary worth keeping is that P alone is fast but imprecise, I alone is precise but slow and prone to overshoot, and D alone does nothing without the others to dampen. Nearly every industrial temperature, flow, and pressure control loop is some flavour of PID underneath.

From Sensor to Network

Instrumentation

Once a reading is digitised, it still has to get to wherever it's used — a PLC, a SCADA screen, a data logger. A 4–20mA loop carries exactly one value per pair of wires; getting several registers' worth of data (multiple sensor channels, status flags, setpoints) over one cable is what a fieldbus protocol is for. This Handbook's Modbus RTU/TCP tutorial picks up exactly here — the same opto-isolated relay board and current-loop transmitters discussed in this tutorial are, in practice, very often read out over an RS-485 Modbus network rather than wired point-to-point.

● Practice Exercises
Basic Size a current-limiting resistor for an LED

An LED needs 20mA and drops 2V forward voltage. It's powered from a 9V supply through a single series resistor. What resistor value is needed?

Answer The resistor must drop the remaining voltage: 9V − 2V = 7V, at 0.02A. R = V/I = 7/0.02 = 350Ω. In practice you'd pick the nearest standard value above this (e.g. 360Ω or 390Ω) so the LED never exceeds its rated current.
Basic Combine two resistors in parallel

What's the combined resistance of a 100Ω and a 200Ω resistor wired in parallel?

Answer (100 × 200) / (100 + 200) = 20000/300 = 66.7Ω. As always with parallel resistors, the result is smaller than either individual resistor.
Intermediate Find an RC low-pass cutoff frequency

An anti-aliasing filter uses R = 4.7kΩ and C = 10nF. What's its −3dB cutoff frequency?

Answer fc = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 4700 × 10×10−&sup9;) ≈ 3386Hz. Any signal content above roughly 3.4kHz is attenuated before it reaches the ADC.
Intermediate Choose a feedback resistor for a target op-amp gain

A non-inverting amplifier needs a gain of 21×, and Rin is fixed at 1kΩ. What value of Rf is needed?

Answer From Av = 1 + Rf/Rin: 21 = 1 + Rf/1000 → Rf = 20 × 1000 = 20,000Ω (20kΩ).
Advanced Decode a 4–20mA reading with an offset range

A loop-powered transmitter is calibrated −20°C to +80°C over its 4–20mA span. The loop currently reads 14mA. What temperature does that represent?

Answer Fraction of span: (14−4)/16 = 0.625. Applied to the range: −20 + 0.625 × (80−(−20)) = −20 + 0.625×100 = 42.5°C.
Advanced Back out strain from a Wheatstone bridge reading

A single-active-arm Wheatstone bridge is excited with Vex = 10V, and the measured output is 5mV. What's the fractional resistance change (ΔR/R) in the active gauge?

Answer Rearranging Vout ≈ (Vex/4) × (ΔR/R): ΔR/R = Vout × 4 / Vex = 0.005 × 4 / 10 = 0.002, i.e. a 0.2% resistance change — a realistic order of magnitude for a loaded strain gauge.
